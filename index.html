Level 1: Who Lives Where?

Demography is the study of populations. Three critical parts are fertility, mortality, and migration.

Fertility
Births within a population.
Mortality
Deaths within a population.
Immigration
Movement into an area for permanent residence.
Emigration
Movement out of an area for permanent residence.
Real World: A town with many young families may need more schools and childcare. A community with an aging population may need different healthcare, transportation, and housing. Population numbers influence real decisions.

Important migration terminology

Refugee: a person forced to leave their country to escape circumstances such as war or persecution.

Asylum-seeker: a person seeking protection whose refugee claim has not yet been validated.

Internally displaced person: someone forced from home who remains within their country's borders.

Level 2: Can Population Grow Forever?

Population theories disagree about what happens when human populations grow and resources are limited.

Malthusian Theory

Thomas Malthus argued that population could grow faster than available resources. Carrying capacity is how many people can live in an area considering available resources. Malthus identified war, famine, and disease as forces that could increase mortality and constrain population.

Scenario: A city has water infrastructure for 500,000 residents but grows toward 900,000. What pressures could appear besides drinking-water shortages?

Cornucopian Theory

Cornucopian theory argues that human ingenuity can solve environmental and social problems. If society needs more food, energy, or another resource, innovation may create new ways to provide it.

Zero Population Growth

Zero population growth (ZPG) occurs when people entering through births and immigration are balanced by people leaving through deaths and emigration.

Memory model: Imagine a bathtub. Births + immigration are water flowing IN. Deaths + emigration are water flowing OUT. If the flows balance, the water level remains relatively stable.

Demographic Transition

Stage 1: high birth and death rates. Stage 2: death rates decline while birth rates remain high. Stage 3: birth rates decline as industrialization develops. Stage 4: low birth and death rates and relative population stability.

A country has improving healthcare, falling infant mortality, longer life expectancy, but birth rates remain high. Which stage best fits?

Theory Showdown: A city is running short on clean water. A Malthusian interpretation emphasizes resource limits. A cornucopian interpretation emphasizes innovation and adaptation.

Level 3: Why Do Cities Grow?

Urbanization examines social, political, and economic relationships in cities. Industrialization helped drive U.S. urban growth as people moved toward factory jobs and other opportunities.

But growth also creates demand for housing, roads, sanitation, schools, food, water, transportation, and employment.

Suburb
Community surrounding a city, generally within commuting distance.
Exurb
Community beyond the suburban ring, often involving longer commutes.
Metropolis
A city and its surrounding suburbs and communities.
Megalopolis
A very large urban corridor involving multiple metropolitan areas.

The Hidden Cost of More Space

A person moves farther from a city for a larger home and yard, but now drives 45 minutes each way to work. One commute is an individual choice. Multiply it by 100,000 commuters and it becomes a social pattern.

Which effects could widespread suburban or exurban growth contribute to?

Gentrification

Gentrification occurs when middle- and upper-class residents enter and renovate historically less affluent city areas while rising price pressures can push working-poor residents elsewhere.

Neighborhood timeline: New businesses arrive → buildings are renovated → property values rise → rents increase → some longtime residents can no longer afford to stay.

Level 4: Put On Your Sociological Lenses

The same issue looks different depending on the question a sociologist asks.

Functionalism
How do different parts of society work together? What functions help maintain social life?
Conflict Theory
Who has power? Who gets resources? Who benefits, and who experiences costs?
Symbolic Interactionism
How do everyday interactions, labels, language, and meanings shape experience?

Level 5: People + Environment

Environmental sociology examines how humans interact with their environments. Population, cities, resource use, and environmental consequences are connected.

Population → Resources → Environment → Society → Population

Case Study: Fracking

Hydraulic fracturing, or fracking, uses high-pressure water, sand, and chemicals to extract oil and natural gas from rock. The chapter presents it as a social tradeoff rather than a simple one-sided issue.

Potential benefits
Energy production, economic activity, employment, access to reserves, and potentially lower energy costs.
Potential concerns
Large water use, possible contamination, environmental effects, induced seismic activity, and continued reliance on fossil fuels.
Community Meeting: An energy company proposes drilling near your community and promises jobs and tax revenue. Residents are also concerned about environmental risks.

Environmental Inequality

Conflict theorists may ask whether environmental risks and political influence are distributed equally. Imagine two neighborhoods opposing an industrial facility. One has wealthy residents, attorneys, business owners, and strong political connections. The other has fewer economic and political resources.
